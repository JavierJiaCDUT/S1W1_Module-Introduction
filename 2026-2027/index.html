S1 / WEEK 01 · MODULE INTRODUCTION12–22 min / 08
Make the distinctionCHC5056 / 2026–2027

The Project Creates and Improves the Product.

PROJECT

The Organised Work

A temporary effort to design, build and evaluate a campus queue service.
People · time · decisions · delivery
PRODUCT

The Result People Use

The queue service that helps students know when to return.
A system · a service · an experience
Project work → Product → Use and improvement

Finishing the work does not, by itself, prove that users benefit.

A project has a defined purpose and a beginning and end. A product can continue to be used and improved after a particular project ends. In computing, a product can be software, a system or a service. The same distinction applies to the campus queue example.

Follow the value chainCHC5056 / 2026–2027

A Feature Is Not a Benefit.

USER PROBLEM

Students wait at the desk because they do not know when they will be called.

FEATURE / OUTPUT

A Queue Alert

What we build and deliver.
→
OUTCOME

Less Waiting at the Desk

The change we want to observe.
→
BENEFIT

More Control over Time

Why that change matters to students.

These are intended effects in a teaching scenario. Evidence is needed to establish whether they occur.

A feature describes something a product can do. An output is a delivered result, such as the implemented alert. An outcome is a change resulting from use. A benefit is an improvement valued by a stakeholder. The chain is a hypothesis until evidence supports the connections.

S1 / WEEK 01 · MODULE INTRODUCTION22–32 min / 10
Stakeholder perspectivesCHC5056 / 2026–2027

Value for Whom?

STUDENT

“Let Me Use My Time Well.”

Wants a reliable estimate and enough notice to return.
Possible indicator: time spent waiting at the desk
SERVICE MANAGER

“Keep the Service Moving.”

Wants students to return on time and staff capacity to be used well.
Possible indicator: missed appointments
Think It ThroughCould less waiting for students create more missed appointments?
Check both perspectives before claiming success.

Stakeholders can value different changes. An alert sent too early might reduce time at the desk but increase missed appointments. These are hypothetical effects. Check outcomes for affected stakeholders rather than choosing a single convenient success measure.

S1 / WEEK 01 · MODULE INTRODUCTION22–32 min / 11
Retrieval check 02CHC5056 / 2026–2027

Classify the Claim.

Use each label once: project, feature/output, outcome, benefit, assumption.

  1. Build and evaluate a queue service over six weeks.
  2. The service sends a call-back alert.
  3. Students spend less time waiting at the desk.
  4. Students have more control over their time.
  5. Students will notice the alert and return in time.
Explore the Reasoning
1 Project · 2 Feature/output · 3 Outcome · 4 Benefit · 5 Assumption.

Statement 3 needs observation; statement 5 needs investigation. Correct this claim: “Sending an alert proves that students benefit.”

The statements describe a hypothetical product; they are not verified findings. An outcome is a change, while a benefit explains why the change matters. The claim about returning in time is an assumption that could fail even when the alert feature works.

New Evidence Should Change the Plan.

or a Return Window.

Adaptive delivery still involves planning. Evidence helps us revise it.

The trial is hypothetical. When important needs or solutions are uncertain, a small step can expose a weak assumption before extensive development. A predictive approach plans more of the work in advance where requirements are sufficiently understood; this example illustrates why an adaptive approach can help. It does not require detailed Scrum knowledge.

Explore the Reasoning
One possible rewrite: Students visiting the advice desk do not know when they will be seen. A queue-notification service could help them use their waiting time more freely. We need to find out whether they can return reliably after an alert. The solution does not need AI unless evidence justifies it.

Keep this four-line project-interest card for your Seminar. Identify a possible benefit, not a promise that the product will succeed. No final project choice is assessed in this lecture. Give peer feedback on specificity and reasoning, not on how technically impressive the idea sounds.
